# Evaluating Decipherments

This section sets out a **proposed protocol**, synthesized here; no tests were run. It lets a reader compare linguistic and non-linguistic proposals without assuming either.

## Distinguish three claims
1. **Structure detected:** sign sequences are non-random. Rao et al. (2009) and Yadav et al. (2010) report sign-order regularities in Indus sequences; Yadav et al. state they cannot confirm natural language.
2. **Language demonstrated:** the structure arises from a spoken language. Rao et al. argue only that this becomes more probable; Sproat (2010) disputes that such statistics separate language from non-linguistic systems.
3. **Reading recovered:** specific sign values are justified. Structure alone cannot show this.

## Proposed checks
Each check names an outcome that would weaken a proposal.
1. **Corpus and segmentation:** publish the corpus source, sign-variant merging and text-boundary rules. *Weakened if* conclusions change under reasonable alternative sign lists.
2. **Consistent rules:** fix sign-to-value rules in advance, with no per-inscription rebus choices. *Weakened if* the same sign needs different values in similar contexts, or readings need many ad hoc exceptions.
3. **Held-out prediction:** develop the proposal on part of the corpus, then predict withheld inscriptions. *Weakened if* accuracy is no better than a baseline model.
4. **Independent corroboration:** seek archaeological or cross-text support, such as a bilingual text or matching artifact types. *Weakened if* readings conflict with find contexts.
5. **Fair controls:** apply the same statistic to known linguistic and non-linguistic systems. *Weakened if* a non-linguistic control scores as language does.

## Limits
- Short texts give small samples, so estimated statistics are unstable (Yadav et al. 2010 describe the corpus as small).
- Flexible rebus lets many readings fit; fit alone is weak evidence.
- Statistical regularity can arise from non-linguistic sources (Sproat 2010).
