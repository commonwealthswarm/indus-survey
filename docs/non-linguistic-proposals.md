# Non-Linguistic Interpretations of Indus Signs

A writing system that encodes spoken language lets a reader recover words or sounds. A non-linguistic symbol system conveys meaning without being tied to speech, as emblems or marks do. Non-linguistic does not mean meaningless; such symbols can carry religious, political or economic meaning.

## The central case

Farmer, Sproat and Witzel (2004) argue that the Indus signs were not a script. Their evidence:

- **Observation:** inscriptions are extremely short. The longest on one surface has 17 signs, and the mean is 4.6 signs across the 2,905 objects in Mahadevan's concordance (Farmer et al. 2004).
- **Comparison:** they report that most of the roughly 21 known Linear Elamite inscriptions are longer than the longest Indus text, and compare the Indus signs to Near Eastern emblem systems, which they say served religious, political and social functions (Farmer et al. 2004).
- **Interpretation:** they propose the signs were religious or political symbols rather than speech (Farmer et al. 2004; as summarised by Rao et al. 2010).

Brevity is an observation. Reading it as evidence against language is the authors' interpretation.

## Responses and limits

Rao et al. (2010) reply to Sproat (2010) in *Computational Linguistics*. They state that scholars have voiced strong disagreement with the 2004 thesis and that several published point-by-point rebuttals, naming Parpola (2005) among them. This survey did not read those rebuttals and reports them only as described by Rao et al. (2010). Rao et al. (2010) also argue that the conditional-entropy evidence discussed in their earlier work is consistent with linguistic structure; the evaluation section covers what such statistics can and cannot show.

Inability to decipher the signs does not show they are non-linguistic, and symbolic function alone does not either. Neither side has produced an accepted reading.
