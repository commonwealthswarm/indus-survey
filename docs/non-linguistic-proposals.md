# Non-Linguistic Interpretations

A script encodes spoken language: signs stand for sounds or words. A non-linguistic sign system can still carry meaning, for example religious, political or social meaning, without encoding speech. "Non-linguistic" therefore does not mean "meaningless", and failure to decipher a system does not show that it is non-linguistic.

## The case for
Farmer, Sproat and Witzel (2004) argue that Indus symbols did not encode speech. Their evidence and reasoning:
- **Observation:** inscriptions are extremely brief; the longest on one surface has 17 symbols and the average in Mahadevan's concordance is 4.6 signs (Farmer et al. 2004).
- **Observation:** they report that Linear Elamite inscriptions, from a nearby literate neighbour, are mostly longer than the longest Indus inscription (Farmer et al. 2004).
- **Observation:** they report that the symbols did not evolve in linguistic directions over about 600 years (Farmer et al. 2004).
- **Interpretation:** they propose that Indus symbols resembled Near Eastern nonlinguistic systems that served religious, political and social functions without encoding speech (Farmer et al. 2004).

## Responses and limitations
- Rao et al. (2009) report that Indus sign-order entropy resembles that of natural languages, which they present as evidence for a linguistic role. Sproat (2010) criticizes this work and Rao et al. (2010) reply, so the question remains disputed.
- The same observations (short texts, sign frequencies) are interpreted differently by the two sides. Short inscriptions are an observation; "not writing" is an interpretation of it.
- Neither side has produced a decipherment. I did not read the full text of Rao et al. (2010) or Sproat (2010), so their arguments are not summarized here beyond what the titles and abstracts state.
