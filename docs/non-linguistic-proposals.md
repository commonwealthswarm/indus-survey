# Non-Linguistic Interpretations

A script encodes spoken language: signs map to sounds or words. A non-linguistic sign system can still carry meaning, for example as emblems of identity, ownership or status, without recording speech. "Non-linguistic" therefore does not mean "meaningless", and failure to decipher a system does not show it is non-linguistic.

## The case for
Farmer, Sproat and Witzel (2004) argue the Indus signs were not a sound-coding writing system and that Harappan literacy was assumed rather than demonstrated. Their evidence:
- **Observation:** inscriptions are very short; the longest on a single surface has about 17 signs (Farmer et al. 2004).
- **Observation:** they argue contemporary scripts, such as the roughly 50-sign Linear Elamite inscription, show many repeated signs, whereas the Indus corpus does not (Farmer et al. 2004).
- **Interpretation:** the authors read these patterns as closer to non-linguistic symbol systems, such as religious or political emblems, than to writing.
The authors offered a prize for an inscription of at least 50 signs showing script-like repetition, and listed other findings that would falsify their model, such as a bilingual text (Farmer 2004–2007).

## Responses and limitations
- Rao et al. (online rebuttal) reply that absence of long texts is not evidence of absence: writing on perishable materials like palm leaves would rarely survive, and some accepted early West Asian scripts have comparable text-length statistics.
- Rao et al. (2009) report that Indus sign-sequence entropy resembles natural languages, which they say raises the probability that the script is linguistic. Sproat (2010) disputes that inference, so the question remains contested.
- Both sides interpret shared observations (short texts, sign frequencies) differently; neither side has a decipherment.
